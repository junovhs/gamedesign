# THE LITTLE DIGGER

## Game Concept

**Working title**

**Genre:** Treasure-hunting adventure
**Perspective:** Third-person
**Mode:** Single-player
**Platforms:** PC and console

---

# PAGE 1 — THE GAME

## Overview

*The Little Digger* is a third-person adventure about exploring a beautiful fantasy world with a small creature that can sense treasure underground.

The player begins with a plain, young creature and a simple home in the countryside. The creature is cute, curious, clumsy, and full of personality. It can run, jump, sniff out buried objects, and dig.

The basic experience is easy to understand:

**Go somewhere interesting. Search for treasure. Dig it up. Bring it home. Use what you found to reach somewhere new.**

When the creature senses treasure, it reacts through sound and movement. Its ears rise. It turns toward the signal. It becomes more excited as the player gets closer.

When the player begins a treasure search, the music starts and a timer appears. The player races around the area, following the creature’s reactions and digging wherever the signal seems strongest.

Most holes contain something useful or amusing:

* Coins
* Food
* Tools
* Old objects
* Decorations
* Strange materials
* Pieces of larger collections

Sometimes the player finds a treasure map.

That is the big moment.

A treasure map is mainly a picture. It might show a crooked tree beside a bridge, a tower during a storm, or a small island beneath a large moon. The player studies the image, remembers where they have seen something similar, and goes looking.

Finding one treasure map during a search feels exciting.

Finding two in the same search should feel incredible.

The rarest discoveries change the game. They may improve the creature, open a route, repair something at home, or reveal a completely new place.

The player begins by digging through fields and beaches. Later, the creature learns to swim, climb, glide, and reach places that once seemed impossible.

Eventually, the player explores ruins beneath the sea, hidden valleys, moving buildings, floating islands, and a final place high above the clouds.

## The Main Feeling

The game is about the pleasure of realizing:

* Something valuable could be nearby.
* The creature is becoming excited.
* There may still be enough time to find one more thing.
* A picture on a treasure map looks familiar.
* A new ability can be used somewhere the player remembers.
* A confusing place has become easy to navigate.
* A small discovery has opened a much larger part of the world.

The player should regularly think:

**“Wait—I know where that is.”**

Or:

**“Now I can finally get up there.”**

Or:

**“I already found one map, but I think I can get another.”**

## Simple Play, Deep World

The game should have very low cognitive load at any one time.

The player is rarely asked to manage several problems, menus, or objectives at once. Most moments revolve around one clear question:

* Where is the treasure?
* How do I reach that place?
* Can I avoid being seen?
* Should I keep searching or go home?
* What happens if I use this here?

The player’s main actions are simple:

* Move
* Jump
* Listen
* Dig
* Interact
* Use one item
* Sneak or distract when necessary

The depth comes from how the world responds to those actions.

A piece of fruit can feed an animal, distract someone, weigh down a switch, leave a scent trail, or cause a large creature to move. The player does not need to learn five different systems. They only need to understand that fruit can be thrown.

The game should feel easy to begin and satisfying to master.

## The Creature

The creature is the emotional center of the game.

It should be appealing even when the player is doing nothing important. It chases insects, shakes dirt from its body, watches distant objects, naps in strange positions, and reacts to the world around it.

Its treasure sense is communicated almost entirely through animation and sound.

Far from treasure, it may give a small uncertain chirp.

Closer, it pulls toward the signal and becomes more alert.

Very close, it can barely contain itself.

Directly above something valuable, it erupts into an unmistakable excited reaction.

The player should learn to read the creature naturally, without needing meters, written explanations, or a long tutorial.

Movement should also feel good before rewards are added. The creature has weight and momentum, but it remains easy to control. It leans into turns, skids on loose ground, braces before digging, tumbles after large jumps, and quickly recovers from mistakes.

The controls, animation, sound, dirt, dust, and camera movement all work together to make searching and digging satisfying.

# PAGE 2 — EXPLORATION AND PROGRESSION

## The World

The game takes place in a connected fantasy world made from several large, handcrafted areas.

Each area should be visually memorable and enjoyable to visit even when the player is not following a formal objective.

Possible areas include:

* A sunny farming valley
* A rocky coast
* A forest filled with enormous plants
* A town built around old machinery
* A desert crossed by moving ruins
* A hotel carried on the back of a giant animal
* A flooded palace
* A range of floating islands
* A place above the clouds

Each area contains obvious paths, hidden paths, buried treasure, people and creatures with simple routines, and places the player cannot reach yet.

The world should constantly show the player things before explaining them.

Early in the game, the player may see:

* A chest beneath clear water
* A cave halfway up a cliff
* A locked garden
* A strange machine with a missing part
* A distant island moving through the sky
* A treasure signal coming from the other side of a wall
* A door large enough for something enormous

These are not presented as a checklist.

The player simply notices them.

Later, when the creature learns to swim or climb, the player remembers those places and returns.

## Learning Without Studying

The world has patterns, but the player should not feel as though they are doing homework.

A guard takes a lunch break.

A tide goes out in the evening.

A large animal visits the river when it rains.

A cart travels between two floors.

A group of glowing insects gathers near buried treasure.

The game does not need to explain these as named systems. The player sees them happen.

A casual player can follow the most obvious path and still succeed.

A curious player can notice patterns and create a better plan.

A skilled player can combine several pieces of knowledge and recover treasure quickly, quietly, or in an unusual way.

This is where the game takes inspiration from places that become more satisfying as the player learns them.

At first, a large area feels confusing.

After several visits, the player knows the shortcuts, hiding places, useful objects, safe routes, and best digging spots.

The place has not become smaller. The player has become better.

## Treasure Searching

Treasure searches happen in contained parts of the larger world.

When a search begins, music and a timer create urgency. The player moves quickly through the area and uses the creature’s reactions to choose where to dig.

Searching should create constant small decisions:

* Dig now or move closer?
* Finish opening this hole or chase a stronger signal?
* Take the nearby treasure or cross the area for a better one?
* Leave with a treasure map or risk the remaining time?
* Use a valuable item to save a few seconds?

The player should be able to improve through practice.

Beginners follow the loudest signal.

Experienced players read small changes in animation, understand the shape of the area, plan efficient routes, and know which surfaces are likely to hide better treasure.

Different areas make searching feel different.

On a beach, waves may temporarily cover parts of the ground.

In a garden, the player may need to search without being noticed.

In a cave, sound may bounce and make signals less precise.

On a moving platform, the entire search area may slowly change.

In the clouds, treasure may drift rather than remain buried in one place.

The basic action remains the same, but the surroundings create variety.

## Treasure Maps

Treasure maps are the main long-term reward.

They should be visual, exciting, and easy to understand.

Early maps show familiar places clearly.

Later maps may show:

* A place at a certain time of day
* A landmark from an unusual angle
* Two distant features that must line up
* A place during rain or low tide
* A location the player has seen but cannot yet reach
* A place that does not appear to exist

Maps do not need a special name, rarity system, lore category, or long description.

They are pictures of treasure locations.

The player looks at them and wonders where they lead.

## Growing Abilities

The creature gains a small number of major abilities over the course of the game.

These abilities should be visible and easy to understand.

### Swimming

The creature becomes comfortable in water. It can cross rivers, dive, search underwater, and enter flooded passages.

### Climbing

Its claws become stronger. It can climb rough walls, reach rooftops, and break through weak stone.

### Moving Through Plants

It becomes better at hiding in plants, following scents, and interacting with wildlife.

### Gliding

Its ears or fins grow large enough to catch the air. It can glide across gaps and ride rising wind.

### Flying

Near the end of the game, it gains the ability to travel through the sky and reach the most distant places.

These abilities should not require the player to choose a form from a menu.

Once the creature can swim, it swims.

Once it can climb, it climbs.

The player should spend time exploring, not configuring the creature.

The starting abilities also remain useful. The creature may always dig fastest on ordinary ground and fit through spaces that later movement abilities cannot solve.

# PAGE 3 — HOME, CHALLENGES, AND THE FULL JOURNEY

## Home

The player returns to the same home throughout the game.

At first, it is a small camp with a place to sleep, a chest, and a rough table for treasure maps.

As the player finds important objects, the home grows.

A bridge is repaired.

A small shop opens.

A boat appears at the shore.

A garden begins producing useful fruit.

An old machine starts working.

A tower is built for reaching the sky.

New characters and creatures move in.

Collections appear physically in the world.

The home should show the player’s progress without becoming a complicated management game.

The player does not need to manage workers, production timers, several currencies, research points, or long upgrade trees.

Important discoveries should cause clear changes automatically or through one simple choice.

The home becomes more useful because of what the player has found.

It also becomes more personal because it contains memories from the journey.

## Tools and Preparation

Preparation should remain light.

The player normally chooses a destination and leaves.

Before some trips, the player may choose one item to bring:

* Fruit
* A rope
* A noise-making toy
* Something that creates smoke
* A light
* A disguise
* A tool for opening old machines

Each item should have several possible uses, but the player should never face a large inventory-management problem before every trip.

The best tools are understandable immediately.

A rope connects two points.

Fruit attracts creatures.

Smoke blocks sight.

A bell creates noise.

The player discovers clever uses through play.

## Nonlethal Fantasy Conflict

Some areas contain guards, rival treasure hunters, dangerous creatures, or other obstacles.

The game can include physical and satisfying fantasy conflict, but it is not about killing.

The player and creature can:

* Knock someone into a pile of hay
* Push a guard into mud
* Trap a pursuer in sticky plants
* Distract people with food or noise
* Drop harmless objects onto helmets
* Hide inside carts or baskets
* Send someone rolling away in a barrel
* Put creatures to sleep
* Temporarily turn an enemy into a frog
* Create a chain reaction that clears an area

Conflict should feel playful, powerful, and creative.

The player may choose stealth, distraction, speed, environmental tricks, or direct slapstick action.

Being caught does not normally end the game. It creates a problem.

The player may be removed from a restricted area, lose time, drop unsecured treasure, or need to escape.

The goal is to improvise and continue.

## Risk and Failure

The player should sometimes face a clear choice between safety and greed.

They may already have a valuable treasure map when they hear another strong signal.

Their bag may be nearly full.

A storm may be approaching.

A guard may be returning.

The exit may soon close.

The player chooses whether to leave or continue searching.

Failure should matter enough to create tension, but it should not erase major progress.

The player might lose:

* Treasure found during the current trip
* A carried item
* Some money
* A temporary opportunity

The player keeps:

* Major abilities
* Places already reached
* Important home improvements
* Completed collections
* Main story progress

A failed trip should make the player think, “I can do that better next time,” rather than, “I have lost hours of progress.”

## Example Area: The Moving Hotel

One major area is a beautiful hotel built on the back of a huge, slow-moving creature.

The hotel contains guest rooms, gardens, kitchens, laundry passages, a ballroom, a rooftop, and hidden spaces inside the creature’s shell.

The player hears that treasure is buried beneath the ballroom.

The immediate problem is simple:

**How do I get underneath it?**

The player may discover that:

* Laundry carts move between floors.
* The ballroom empties during a performance.
* Music makes buried crystals glow.
* The hotel goes underwater at night.
* A hidden passage opens while it is submerged.
* A large dessert makes the creature sneeze and shifts the furniture.
* The roof passes close to a floating island in certain places.

The player does not receive a list explaining all of this.

They see events, follow interesting people, try objects, and gradually understand the place.

A player who wants a direct route can follow an obvious clue.

A player who enjoys mastery can find several different ways beneath the ballroom.

The treasure map found there shows the hotel beneath a bright group of stars with a small island above it.

Much later, the player realizes the picture shows when and where the hotel passes close enough to reach that island.

One treasure leads naturally to the next part of the world.

## The Full Journey

The game begins in ordinary fields with a plain creature and simple buried objects.

Each major discovery expands what the player can do and where they can go.

The journey gradually moves through:

**Fields → coast → forest → mountains → deep water → strange moving places → floating islands → clouds**

The world becomes larger, more beautiful, and more surprising over time.

The final part of the game takes place above the clouds.

The player reaches a hidden home of enormous ancient creatures related to their small companion. The creature gains its final ability, and routes that once seemed separate become connected.

The ending should feel like the natural result of everything the player has learned and found.

The player began by following random squeaks and digging in the dirt.

They finish by understanding the hidden paths of the entire world.

## Design Promise

*The Little Digger* should never feel like a game the player must study before they can enjoy it.

The controls are simple.

The immediate goal is clear.

The creature communicates through sound and movement.

Treasure maps are pictures.

Progress is visible.

The world quietly contains more depth than the player needs at any one time.

A new player can enjoy running around and finding things.

An experienced player can move through familiar places with confidence, combine simple tools in clever ways, and chase difficult rewards.

The final experience should feel warm, exciting, surprising, and deeply satisfying:

**A cute creature. A beautiful world. A treasure nearby. One more place to reach.**
