# Spencer’s Game Design Philosophy and Player Preferences

## Purpose of this document

This document records the games, mechanics, structures, sensations, and design qualities that Spencer consistently finds compelling. It is not a specification for a single game and is not meant to require every future project to contain every preference listed here.

It is a north star: a description of the kinds of experiences that reliably create curiosity, absorption, mastery, and delight for Spencer.

---

# Part I: Games Spencer Likes and What He Likes About Them

## Stardew Valley

Spencer’s enjoyment of *Stardew Valley* is not limited to its cozy presentation or farming theme. The deeper appeal is the way the game turns an initially open, uncertain world into a network of understandable and optimizable systems.

He enjoys:

- Exploring the valley and gradually learning what exists, where it is, and when it matters.
- Descending through the mines and encountering new enemies, materials, and possibilities.
- Fishing for every species and improving the tools, bait, timing, and knowledge required to do so.
- Cooking, farming, crafting, and building relationships between systems.
- Planning dependency chains such as: to obtain A, he first needs B, which requires C and D.
- Designing an efficient farm rather than merely decorating one.
- Turning early scarcity into abundance, wealth, capability, and control.
- Completing collections when the collected things reveal systems, unlock rewards, or represent mastery.

The pleasure comes from converting uncertainty into knowledge and knowledge into a functioning, increasingly powerful personal world.

## Final Fantasy IX

*Final Fantasy IX* is one of the clearest examples of Spencer’s preferences working together.

### Chocobo Hot and Cold

This is a particularly strong fit because it creates a repeating cycle of curiosity and expansion:

- Search for buried clues.
- Discover Chocographs.
- Interpret those clues in the larger world.
- Travel to likely locations.
- Find hidden treasure.
- Improve the Chocobo’s capabilities.
- Reach places that were previously visible but inaccessible.
- Discover still more secrets.

The system makes exploration useful, collection meaningful, and progression spatially transformative. It repeatedly creates the feeling that the world contains more than it first reveals.

### Equipment and abilities

Spencer likes that equipment is not merely a temporary numerical upgrade. Equipment teaches permanent abilities, so every item can become part of a longer-term plan. Finding, buying, stealing, synthesizing, and equipping items creates overlapping decisions about present strength and future development.

### Synthesis

The synthesis system is appealing because it converts equipment into a network of dependencies. An item is not isolated; it may be an ingredient, a prerequisite, a strategic choice, or part of a route toward something more valuable.

### Stealing from bosses

Spencer repeatedly stole rare equipment from bosses and was willing to reload saves to obtain it. The attraction is not only completion. It is the knowledge that a valuable opportunity exists inside a difficult encounter and can be claimed through preparation, persistence, and system understanding.

### Collections and hidden systems

Spencer strongly enjoyed:

- Chocographs and buried treasure.
- Tetra Master cards.
- Quina’s Blue Magic.
- Frog catching.
- Rare equipment.
- Stellazzio coins.
- Friendly monster encounters.
- Auction-house items.
- Treasure Hunter Rank.
- Character abilities.
- Optional bosses.
- Ultimate weapons.
- Ozma as a culmination of preparation and system mastery.

Mognet letters were less compelling because they did not create the same degree of capability, mechanical payoff, or strategic consequence.

This distinction matters: Spencer does not automatically enjoy collecting for its own sake. A collection becomes especially compelling when each acquisition changes what he can do, reveals another layer of the world, or contributes toward a meaningful culmination.

## Final Fantasy X and the Monster Arena

Spencer especially remembers the Monster Arena. Its appeal lies in the combination of collecting, preparation, hidden requirements, and escalating optional challenges.

Capturing creatures is not merely a checklist. It feeds a larger system that unlocks increasingly unusual and difficult encounters. Progress requires knowledge of locations, enemy types, equipment, builds, and combat strategy. The system rewards thoroughness and eventually validates it through powerful endgame challenges.

This is a strong example of Spencer enjoying a collection because it becomes infrastructure for mastery.

## Cyberpunk 2077

Spencer enjoys wandering through Night City and allowing visual curiosity to pull him toward activity. A street, building, marker, unusual location, or distant detail can become the start of a self-directed objective.

He enjoys:

- Discovering missions while moving through the world rather than selecting everything from a detached menu.
- Studying an encounter and planning a stealth approach.
- Improvising when the plan breaks down.
- Finding loot with meaningful mechanical differences.
- Searching for faster vehicles and more capable equipment.
- Reaching hidden or advanced locations earlier than expected.
- Learning efficient routes for progression and money-making.
- Gradually becoming more mobile, informed, dangerous, and independent.

The game is strongest for him when the city feels like a physical system full of opportunities rather than a backdrop for scripted content.

## Red Dead Redemption 2

Spencer likes *Red Dead Redemption 2* as a world to inhabit, study, and gradually understand.

He enjoys:

- Riding into the wilderness without knowing exactly what he will find.
- Discovering animals, strangers, events, locations, and unusual situations.
- Hunting rare creatures for rewards with practical or symbolic value.
- Seeking exceptional mounts, such as the fastest or most desirable horse.
- Crafting superior food and preparing for travel or danger.
- Robbing trains and taking other optional high-risk, high-reward actions.
- Completing difficult challenges that require broad familiarity with the world.
- Spending time in a beautiful place even when not pursuing a formal objective.

The world rewards attention. Curiosity often produces a story, a resource, a capability, a mystery, or a memorable place.

## Elite Dangerous

Spencer remembers spending roughly seventy hours learning *Elite Dangerous* before becoming deeply engaged. That long learning period was acceptable because the game eventually produced a strong feeling of authentic competence.

He enjoys:

- The scale and physical credibility of space.
- Learning procedures that initially feel difficult and later become natural.
- Harvesting fuel from stars.
- Making long journeys with real spatial and logistical weight.
- Earning better ships, drives, and thrusters.
- Unlocking new kinds of work.
- Mining asteroids.
- Feeling like an actual interstellar pilot rather than a character being carried through a sequence.

The attraction is partly fantasy, but it is also mastery. The game allows knowledge, routine, equipment, and judgment to turn an overwhelming galaxy into a navigable working environment.

## Don’t Starve and Don’t Starve Together

*Don’t Starve* may be one of the purest expressions of Spencer’s preferred world-mastery arc.

He enjoys:

- The art style and strong visual identity.
- Running the map to reveal the coastline and understand the world’s shape.
- Locating important resources before selecting a base site.
- Choosing where to settle based on system knowledge rather than convenience alone.
- Discovering recipes and learning what each resource enables.
- Building farms and long-term production systems.
- Creating emergent automation, such as arranging pig and spider interactions to generate useful materials.
- Exploring caves and deeper layers of the world.
- Adapting to seasons and environmental changes.
- Unlocking crafted items that substantially change what is possible.
- Growing a fragile camp into a large, durable, thriving settlement.
- Maintaining a world long enough that it acquires history and personal meaning.

The initial world is dangerous, opaque, and temporary. Through knowledge and construction, Spencer turns it into something legible, productive, persistent, and partly domesticated.

## Borderlands

Spencer likes the search for larger, stranger, and more mechanically interesting guns. The combination of combat, exploration, and an almost endless possibility space of equipment creates a persistent sense that the next discovery may materially change play.

He enjoys:

- Satisfying combat feedback.
- Weapons that differ in behavior, not only in numerical strength.
- Large spaces that invite detours.
- Loot as a source of experimentation.
- The feeling of becoming increasingly absurd, capable, and specialized.

The appeal is strongest when a new weapon creates a new tactic or a surprising interaction rather than simply adding a small percentage increase.

## Fallout 4 and Fallout: New Vegas

The *Fallout* games align with Spencer’s interest in exploration, systemic freedom, scavenging, build development, and discovering useful things in places that initially appear incidental.

He is drawn to:

- Entering ruins because they look interesting rather than because a quest explicitly requires it.
- Finding weapons, resources, stories, and hidden routes through exploration.
- Building a character whose capabilities reflect accumulated choices.
- Learning which resources matter and seeing formerly mundane objects as useful inputs.
- Solving situations through different combinations of combat, stealth, dialogue, preparation, and knowledge.
- Turning a hostile landscape into a known network of routes, safe areas, suppliers, caches, and opportunities.

*Fallout 4* additionally supports his interest in construction and transforming spaces. *New Vegas* supports his interest in choice, consequence, build identity, and systemic approaches to problems.

## Metal Gear Solid V: The Phantom Pain

*Metal Gear Solid V* fits Spencer’s preference for preparation followed by execution, with room for improvisation.

Its appeal includes:

- Surveying a location before acting.
- Selecting tools and companions for a plan.
- Entering from a chosen direction.
- Using stealth, distraction, timing, and environmental knowledge.
- Recovering valuable people and equipment.
- Developing a persistent base that expands future capability.
- Watching a carefully designed plan collapse and finding a way to recover.

The game allows competence to feel earned. Better outcomes come from understanding guards, terrain, equipment, timing, and systems rather than merely following instructions.

## Dishonored, Deus Ex: Human Revolution, and Hitman

These games share a structure Spencer consistently enjoys: authored spaces that behave like systems.

He is drawn to:

- Observing before committing.
- Finding alternate entrances and hidden routes.
- Seeing locations that suggest possibilities before their purpose is fully understood.
- Combining powers, tools, disguises, stealth, and environmental features.
- Creating a plan and then adapting to consequences.
- Replaying or mentally revisiting a space after learning more about it.
- Feeling that a level is a place to understand rather than a corridor to clear.

The satisfaction comes from reading the environment, forming a theory, acting on it, and seeing the world respond.

## Subnautica

*Subnautica* strongly matches Spencer’s attraction to exploration that is beautiful, useful, and slightly frightening.

Its likely appeal includes:

- Entering unknown regions because something visible suggests that they may contain value or meaning.
- Gathering resources that lead to equipment, vehicles, and deeper access.
- Using new tools to cross previous limits.
- Building bases that make hostile areas feel increasingly habitable.
- Learning ecological and geographic patterns.
- Experiencing dread without losing the desire to continue.
- Discovering that the world has deeper layers, stranger environments, and larger truths.

The game repeatedly turns curiosity into capability and capability into access to more mystery.

## Astroneer and No Man’s Sky

These games support Spencer’s enjoyment of traversal, resource networks, base building, technological progression, and large spaces that can be gradually made more useful.

He is drawn to:

- Reaching new regions and planets.
- Finding materials with clear purposes.
- Building infrastructure.
- Improving mobility and range.
- Creating stable footholds in unfamiliar places.
- Transforming remote environments into connected parts of a personal network.

The scale matters, but only when the player can form meaningful relationships with places and systems within that scale.

## Graveyard Keeper, Cult of the Lamb, House Flipper, and Fields of Mistria

These games express a more domestic version of the same underlying preference.

Spencer enjoys:

- Improving a persistent space.
- Converting clutter, neglect, or scarcity into order and productivity.
- Learning routines and production chains.
- Balancing multiple kinds of work.
- Watching effort become visible in the environment.
- Moving between planning, gathering, building, organizing, and execution.

The fantasy may be softer than survival or exploration games, but the underlying arc remains similar: understand a place, work with its systems, and leave it more capable than it was before.

## Pokémon

Spencer connects strongly with collection when the collection has tactical and expressive meaning.

The appeal includes:

- Discovering creatures in different places.
- Learning where and how they can be found.
- Building a team from many possible combinations.
- Developing creatures over time.
- Completing a collection that also expands strategic options.
- Encountering rare or unusual forms that create memorable pursuits.

A Pokémon is not merely an entry in a catalogue. It is also a tool, a possibility, a memory, and a component in a larger system.

## Diablo

Spencer enjoys progression when it noticeably changes capability, pace, and strategy.

The appeal includes:

- Building toward synergies.
- Finding items that alter how an ability or build functions.
- Becoming dramatically more effective over time.
- Understanding the relationship between equipment, skills, resources, and enemy difficulty.
- Pursuing rare drops when they enable a meaningful transformation.

Raw numerical growth is less interesting than crossing thresholds where the character begins to operate differently.

## Call of Duty Multiplayer

Spencer’s enjoyment of *Call of Duty* multiplayer reflects his interest in immediate responsiveness, precision, flow, and rapid learning.

He enjoys:

- Tight controls.
- Fast feedback.
- Repeated opportunities to improve.
- Learning maps, sightlines, timing, movement, and weapon behavior.
- Entering a focused state where perception and action become nearly automatic.
- Progression and unlocks that provide goals between matches.

This is a different scale of pleasure from world mastery, but it shares the same underlying pattern: knowledge and practiced control become power.

## Guitar Hero, Thumper, parries, and timing-based combat

These experiences appeal to Spencer because they produce direct embodied mastery.

He enjoys:

- Precise timing.
- Strong audio-visual confirmation.
- Repeated actions that become rhythmic and automatic.
- Clear distinctions between weak, acceptable, and excellent execution.
- The pleasure of reading an incoming pattern and responding correctly.
- Entering flow through a demanding but legible control loop.

The action itself must feel satisfying. External rewards cannot compensate for a weak core interaction.

## Tetris, Bejeweled, Candy Crush, Grindstone, and similar compact systems

Spencer admires games that can be understood as rules before they are understood as genres.

He values:

- A small number of interacting rules.
- Immediate readability.
- Rapid feedback.
- Repeated decisions with different consequences.
- Depth emerging from combinations rather than large content volume.
- A loop that remains interesting without narrative, spectacle, or progression carrying it.

These games demonstrate that a strong game can exist first as an interaction and only later as a theme, world, or product category.

## Baba Is You, Portal, and Into the Breach

These games appeal to Spencer’s systems-thinking side.

He enjoys:

- Discovering that the apparent rules are only part of the real system.
- Reframing a problem rather than merely executing faster.
- Seeing several consequences unfold from one decision.
- Working with small, legible state spaces that still produce surprising complexity.
- Reaching solutions that feel inevitable in hindsight but were not obvious initially.

They create the pleasure of understanding, not merely the pleasure of winning.

## Nintendo games: Mario Kart, Super Mario Sunshine, Paper Mario, and Nintendo’s broader toy-like design

Spencer is drawn to Nintendo’s tendency to begin with a physical interaction, toy, or movement idea and then build a game around it.

He values:

- Controls that are enjoyable before they are useful.
- Movement with personality.
- Strong silhouettes, animation, sound, and tactile feedback.
- Mechanics that invite experimentation without requiring explanation.
- Worlds that feel playful, coherent, and full of authored surprises.
- Systems that are accessible immediately but support increasing skill.

This is closely aligned with Spencer’s own belief that the highest-risk question in a game is often whether its core interaction is intrinsically enjoyable.

## Animal Well, Out There, Alto’s Adventure, Fruit Ninja, Doodle Jump, Flappy Bird, Hearthstone, and Plants vs. Zombies

These games represent several qualities Spencer admires even when their themes and genres differ:

- A clear central interaction.
- Strong identity.
- Immediate comprehension through play.
- Minimal dependence on exposition.
- High output from small input.
- Systems that reveal additional depth over time.
- A focused experience rather than a collection of unrelated features.

They reinforce his belief that a game can begin with one compelling verb, rhythm, rule, or decision and expand outward only after that foundation works.

# Part II: Abstracted Philosophy and Preferences

## 1. Spencer is a systems-driven explorer and completionist

Spencer is most engaged when he enters a world that is initially mysterious, dangerous, disordered, or only partly legible and gradually becomes capable within it.

His ideal relationship with a game world is not passive sightseeing. It is an active process of:

> Discovering → understanding → collecting → building → optimizing → unlocking → mastering → discovering more

Completion is most satisfying when it represents genuine understanding rather than the clearing of arbitrary icons.

## 2. Knowledge becoming power is a central pleasure

A recurring pattern across Spencer’s favorite games is that information has practical force.

Learning where a resource appears, how a creature behaves, what an item enables, how a map connects, which route is safe, what a boss carries, or how two systems interact can materially change play.

He likes becoming powerful because he understands the world, not only because a meter increased.

## 3. Exploration is strongest when it produces mechanical consequences

Spencer enjoys curiosity for its own sake, but curiosity becomes especially compelling when it leads to:

- New capabilities.
- Hidden systems.
- Useful resources.
- Better routes.
- New tools.
- Rare encounters.
- Meaningful collections.
- Strategic information.
- A changed understanding of the world.

A beautiful horizon is more interesting when reaching it matters.

## 4. Visible inaccessibility is especially motivating

Spencer is strongly drawn to things that can be seen, sensed, or inferred before they can be reached.

Examples include:

- A ledge without an obvious route.
- A sealed door.
- A distant island.
- A submerged object.
- An unusual enemy encountered too early.
- A material with no known use.
- A terrain type that current tools cannot cross.

These create unresolved mental loops. Later access feels meaningful because the place or object already existed in the player’s imagination.

## 5. Progression is most satisfying when it is transformative

Spencer prefers progression that changes verbs, routes, tactics, or possibilities.

He is especially drawn to upgrades that allow the player to:

- Move differently.
- Reach new places.
- Survive new environments.
- Automate previous labor.
- Use resources in new ways.
- Combine systems differently.
- Turn a difficult procedure into a mastered routine.

Purely incremental numerical growth is less memorable unless it crosses a threshold that changes how the game feels.

## 6. Collections should carry meaning

Spencer likes collecting, but not all collections are equally compelling.

The strongest collections provide one or more of the following:

- Mechanical utility.
- Strategic options.
- World knowledge.
- Access to hidden content.
- A visible culmination.
- A personal history.
- Proof of mastery.
- Components in a larger system.

A collection is weaker when its entries are interchangeable, purely cosmetic, or detached from the rest of the game.

## 7. Nested dependency chains are intrinsically enjoyable

Spencer enjoys planning through linked requirements:

> To obtain A, first obtain B. B requires C and D. D becomes easier after building E.

These chains create anticipation and make ordinary actions feel connected to a larger purpose. They are especially satisfying when the player discovers the chain, chooses the order, and gradually shortens or automates it.

## 8. Spencer enjoys turning wilderness into infrastructure

Many of Spencer’s favorite experiences involve creating persistent order inside an initially indifferent world.

This may take the form of:

- A farm.
- A survival base.
- A network of routes.
- A ship and its supporting economy.
- A developed settlement.
- A known set of caches and safe locations.
- A roster, collection, or equipment library.

The deeper pleasure is not simply ownership. It is the visible accumulation of understanding and effort.

## 9. Preparation and execution should both matter

Spencer enjoys studying a situation, assembling a plan, and then carrying it out.

He also enjoys when the world is dynamic enough that plans can fail and require improvisation.

This creates a satisfying three-part rhythm:

> Observe → prepare → execute and adapt

A game is especially engaging when both good planning and skillful recovery are valuable.

## 10. Risk is attractive when the reward is legible

Spencer likes optional danger when he understands what may be gained.

Examples include:

- Attempting to steal a rare item from a boss.
- Entering a dangerous region early.
- Robbing a train.
- Hunting a rare creature.
- Reaching a difficult ledge.
- Continuing deeper with limited supplies.

The tension comes from choosing to commit, not from arbitrary punishment.

## 11. Simple rules can support deep mastery

Spencer admires games in which a small ruleset produces a large possibility space.

He is drawn to:

- Interactions that combine in unexpected ways.
- Mechanics that remain useful across many situations.
- Systems that can be understood locally but have broader consequences.
- Repetition that creates learning rather than monotony.
- Depth produced by relationships, not merely by feature count.

This is one reason he resists beginning with a genre label. A genre is a package of expectations; a compelling game can begin with a single interaction.

## 12. The core interaction must be enjoyable without support systems

Spencer believes the largest early uncertainty in many games is whether the central mechanic is intrinsically fun.

Rewards, progression, narrative, content volume, and visual polish can strengthen a good interaction, but they should not be used to conceal a weak one.

A strong prototype allows the player to enjoy the repeated action before the larger game exists.

## 13. Movement, timing, and control can be pleasures in themselves

Spencer enjoys control systems that reward practice and produce flow.

He is drawn to:

- Acceleration and braking.
- Momentum.
- Timing windows.
- Rhythm.
- Positioning.
- Recovery from mistakes.
- Fine adjustments followed by committed action.
- A growing sense of physical fluency.

The player should eventually feel that the system is expressive and dependable, not that they are fighting the interface.

## 14. Responsiveness and “juice” are part of the mechanic

Spencer values games that visibly, audibly, and physically respond to input.

A satisfying action often produces several coordinated reactions:

- Eased movement rather than robotic interpolation.
- Anticipation, overshoot, recoil, and settling.
- Squash, stretch, lean, rotation, and body follow-through.
- Sound with material character.
- Particles, dust, debris, trails, and environmental response.
- Camera motion and screen shake proportional to impact.
- Secondary objects that wobble, sway, or react.
- Personality expressed through animation.

The important quality is not indiscriminate spectacle. It is the feeling that an input entered a living system and caused a cascade of coherent consequences.

For Spencer, this feedback is not merely decoration. It helps communicate weight, speed, force, danger, success, and control.

## 15. Physicality should be readable and cooperative

Spencer is attracted to simulation and believable motion, but not to realism that makes control frustrating or opaque.

He prefers systems that feel physical while remaining understandable:

- Momentum is preserved, but corrections are possible.
- Weight is communicated, but movement is not sluggish.
- Collisions matter, but do not feel arbitrary.
- Animation follows forces, but does not obscure state.
- The player can recognize why an outcome occurred.

The ideal response to failure is “I misjudged that,” not “the game behaved unpredictably.”

## 16. Multiple cognitive modes can support one unified experience

Spencer often enjoys games that let him move among:

- Exploration.
- Combat.
- Planning.
- Building.
- Crafting.
- Collecting.
- Organizing.
- Optimization.
- Quiet observation.

The appeal is not variety for its own sake. These modes are strongest when they all contribute to the same persistent project or world.

## 17. Atmosphere and visual identity are prerequisites for deep attachment

Spencer is much more likely to invest deeply in a game when he loves being in its world.

He values:

- A strong art direction.
- Memorable places.
- A readable visual language.
- Beauty mixed with mystery.
- Charm, humor, dread, or melancholy used with confidence.
- A world that feels authored rather than generic.

Presentation cannot replace mechanics, but it strongly affects whether he wants to remain in the experience long enough to master them.

## 18. Mystery works best when it promises structure beneath the surface

Spencer is drawn to worlds that suggest hidden depth:

- An impossible place glimpsed at the edge of a believable one.
- A strange object with no immediate explanation.
- A route that continues beyond current access.
- A system whose full purpose is not yet clear.
- A quiet sign that the world is larger or stranger than expected.

The mystery is strongest when later discovery reveals relationships rather than only lore.

## 19. Focus matters more than feature count

Spencer does not want every game to include exploration, crafting, progression, collecting, building, combat, and narrative simultaneously.

He values focused designs that choose a few compatible pleasures and execute them deeply.

A small game can strongly fit his preferences through one excellent mechanic, one compact system, or one satisfying arc of discovery and mastery.

## 20. Spencer is the first customer

Spencer’s design process begins from honest personal interest.

He treats his own enjoyment as initial evidence that a real audience may exist. This does not mean assuming everyone shares his taste. It means designing from direct conviction rather than from a generic idea of what a market category is supposed to contain.

The relevant question is not merely “What games sell?” but:

> What interaction, system, mystery, or world would Spencer genuinely want to keep engaging with after the novelty is gone?

# Compact North Star

Spencer is most drawn to games that let him enter a compelling world, notice something unexplained or unreachable, learn how the world works, gather useful things, build capability, connect systems, take calculated risks, and gradually turn uncertainty into mastery.

He values mechanics that feel good before rewards are added, systems that produce depth from a small number of rules, collections that matter, progression that changes possibilities, and environments that remember the player’s effort.

He especially enjoys the moment when something that was once mysterious, dangerous, inefficient, or inaccessible becomes understandable and usable because of what the player has learned.

His preferred emotional arc is:

> Curiosity → discovery → planning → acquisition → construction → efficiency → confidence → renewed curiosity

His preferred mechanical arc is:

> Learn → practice → control → combine → exploit → master

And his broadest recurring fantasy is:

> Enter an unknown world, understand it deeply, and become capable within it without exhausting its mystery.
